The Importance of Class Struggle in Environmentalism

Why the carbon footprint, and environmentalism more broadly, cannot be understood without asking who holds power.

Summary

Environmentalism is often framed as personal responsibility: recycle, cut your footprint, buy better. This report argues that individual action matters but cannot carry the weight placed on it, because emissions are unevenly produced, the largest decisions are made by institutions, and the harms fall unevenly. It uses the carbon footprint, and BP's role in popularising it, as a case in point.

1Introduction

We are encouraged to recycle, avoid plastic, plant trees, use public transport, conserve electricity and reduce our carbon footprint. These actions can be useful. They also raise a larger question.

Can environmental problems be solved if we only change individual behaviour, without changing the systems that produce environmental damage?

Environmental destruction does not occur independently of society. It is connected to how land, labour, energy, resources and wealth are organised. The effects are unequal too: a wealthy corporation, a middle-class household and a low-income worker have different levels of consumption, economic security, political influence and control over production. They do not have the same environmental impact or the same ability to make environmental decisions.

This report does not argue that individual actions are useless. It explores their limits and asks whether individual responsibility should receive so much attention when many of the largest environmental decisions are made at an institutional and economic level.

3The carbon footprint

A carbon footprint is an estimate of the greenhouse-gas emissions associated with an individual, household, product, organisation or activity. Measuring emissions is useful: it shows where they come from and where they can be cut.

The criticism

The popular focus on the individual footprint has been criticised for steering attention away from larger structural sources of emissions. The issue is not whether personal emissions exist. They do. The issue is why so much attention goes to what individuals consume, rather than to who controls what is produced, how it is produced and which energy is used.

The footprint questionThe system question
What can I personally do to reduce my footprint?What economic and political systems are producing emissions at this scale?
Focus: consumption, households, lifestyleFocus: production, ownership, infrastructure, policy
Responsibility: individualsResponsibility: institutions, firms, governments

What a footprint actually counts

Organisations usually measure emissions using the Greenhouse Gas Protocol, which sorts them into three scopes. The scopes show why the question "whose footprint is it?" is not as simple as it sounds.

ScopeWhat it coversExample for an oil company
Scope 1Direct emissions from sources the organisation owns or controls.Burning fuel at its own refineries and drilling sites.
Scope 2Emissions from the electricity, heat or steam the organisation buys.Power bought to run its facilities.
Scope 3All other emissions in the value chain, upstream and downstream.Customers burning the petrol and gas it sells.

Why the boundary matters

For a fossil fuel producer, the large majority of the emissions linked to its business usually falls under Scope 3, because most of them are released when customers burn the product. This is the basis of an argument that is often made in defence of producers: the emissions belong to the people who use the fuel.

The counter-argument is that producers decide whether to find, extract and market fossil fuels in the first place, and that customers often have few alternatives in the short term. Under this view, responsibility is shared, and assigning all of it to individual users leaves out the choices made higher up the chain.

Both can be true

Individuals do burn fuel, and producers do supply it. The question this report asks is which of the two receives most of the public attention, and which holds more power to change the system.

4The BP campaign

Ideas of measuring environmental impact existed before the modern personal carbon-footprint campaign. What stands out is how the oil company BP helped turn the personal carbon footprint into a household phrase during its "Beyond Petroleum" branding in the 2000s.

YearEvent
2000BP rebrands as "Beyond Petroleum", with a green sunburst logo and a pitch as an energy company.
2004BP works with the advertising agency Ogilvy & Mather and promotes a personal carbon footprint calculator.
2007The campaign wins a gold Effie advertising award.
2010The Deepwater Horizon rig explodes in the Gulf of Mexico, killing 11 workers and spilling millions of barrels of oil.

Why it matters for the argument

The calculator invited people to ask "what is my footprint?" A producer of fossil fuels was, in effect, asking its customers to examine their own consumption. This moved the spotlight from the companies that extract and sell fossil fuels to the people who burn them. BP is itself among the largest investor-owned producers in the Carbon Majors database, which records the firms behind most industrial emissions since 1988.

A careful reading

BP did not invent footprint calculators or the ecological footprint. The claim made by journalists and researchers, including Mark Kaufman and Geoffrey Supran, is that BP popularised the personal version at scale and that the effect was to individualise blame. BP has told reporters it has no records explaining why the campaign was created, so the motive is an interpretation, although the effect is easy to observe.

A familiar pattern: the litter campaign

The BP case is often compared with an earlier example. Keep America Beautiful, an organisation founded in 1953 with backing from beverage and packaging companies, became widely known for anti-litter campaigns, including the 1971 television advertisement known as the "Crying Indian". Campaigns of this kind framed a problem created by the rise of disposable packaging as a question of individual conduct, in that case litter, so that attention moved away from how packaging was designed and sold.

The two cases are not identical, but they share a structure: an industry that profits from a product supports a message that places responsibility with the person who uses it.

What the BP case shows, and what it does not

It does showIt does not show
A major fossil fuel producer promoted the personal footprint as a way of thinking about climate.That measuring a personal footprint is useless or dishonest.
The framing moved attention toward consumption and away from production.That every individual who calculates a footprint is being manipulated.
Corporate communications can shape how a public problem is understood.The motive behind the campaign, which remains an interpretation.

5Emissions and income

Emissions are not spread evenly across people. Studies of global carbon inequality, such as work by Lucas Chancel and by Oxfam, estimate the share of consumption emissions by income group. Figures vary by year and method, but the pattern is consistent.

Richest 10% of peopleabout half of emissions
Richest 1% of peopleroughly 15–17%
Poorest 50% of peopleroughly 8–10%

Per person, the gap is stark: the top 1% emit on the order of seventy times more per year than the bottom half. On the production side, a 2017 report by CDP and the Climate Accountability Institute linked 100 producers to roughly 71% of industrial greenhouse-gas emissions since 1988.

Key idea

Consumption emissions follow income, and production emissions are concentrated in a small number of firms. Both facts point beyond the average household.

Ways of counting emissions

The same world can look different depending on how emissions are counted. Each method answers a different question.

MethodWhat it countsWhat it highlights
Production-basedEmissions released within a territory.Where factories, power stations and farms are located.
Consumption-basedEmissions linked to goods and services used in a territory, including imports.How wealthier countries and households drive emissions that occur elsewhere.
Per personEmissions divided by population.The gap between high and low-consuming groups, including within one country.
HistoricalEmissions added up over many years.Which countries and companies contributed most to the carbon already in the atmosphere.
By producerEmissions traced to the firms that extract and sell fossil fuels.How concentrated the supply of fossil fuels is.

None of these is the single correct view. Together they show that a large share of emissions sits with a small number of wealthy consumers and a small number of producers, which is why the "average footprint" can hide more than it reveals.

The public cost of fossil fuels

In 2023 the International Monetary Fund estimated that global fossil fuel subsidies reached about 7 trillion US dollars in 2022. Most of this was "implicit", meaning the unpriced costs of air pollution and climate damage that are paid by society, with a smaller "explicit" part from direct support and below-cost pricing. Whatever the exact figure, the estimate illustrates the externalisation discussed later in this report: costs of the fossil fuel system are spread across the public.

6When environmentalism becomes a lifestyle

Environmentalism can become depoliticised when it stays at the level of lifestyle choices. The risk is a kind of "gardening problem": activity that looks productive and feels meaningful while leaving the underlying structures of power untouched.

ActionWhat it can achieveWhat it leaves untouched
Plant a treeLocal green space, some carbon uptake, community involvementWho owns and controls the land around it
RecycleLess material sent to landfillHow products are designed and manufactured
Buy organic or localSupports particular producersWho controls the agricultural supply chain
Reusable bottleLess single-use wasteHow water resources are managed

None of these actions is inherently bad. The problem arises when environmentalism is only a collection of lifestyle choices. The argument is not that gardening, recycling or planting trees are worthless. It is that environmentalism cannot stop there.

The demand defence: "we only supply what people want"

Companies often present themselves as the middle of the chain, supplying whatever consumers demand, so that responsibility for what is produced rests with the buyer. This overlooks how much demand is itself shaped by the producers. When a society is organised around particular goods, a life without them becomes hard to imagine, and the "choice" to buy them is less free than it appears.

StepHow it worksExample
Create the needAdvertising, design and fashion cycles turn wants into perceived needs, and new products make earlier ones feel out of date.Yearly product releases, trend cycles in clothing and electronics.
Remove the alternativesInfrastructure, defaults and design make opting out costly in time, money or access.Towns built around cars, disposable packaging as the default, products that are hard or expensive to repair.
Point at the buyerThe company says it is only meeting demand, which moves responsibility from those who decided what was on offer to those who bought it."We just give customers what they want."
A choice you cannot avoid is not much of a choice.
Counterpoint

Consumers do have real preferences, and some choices are genuinely free. Defenders of markets argue that firms succeed only when people want what they sell. The question is how much of demand is shaped by producers and how much is independent of them.

Why individual action still has value

Criticising lifestyle-only environmentalism is different from dismissing personal action. Individual choices can matter in several ways:

  • Direct impact: some personal choices, such as how often a person flies, how they travel and what they eat, carry a large share of a household's emissions.
  • Norms: visible choices can shift what a community considers normal, which can make later policy easier to accept.
  • Markets: collective consumer demand can push companies to offer cleaner options.
  • Political participation: voting, organising and supporting campaigns are also individual actions, and they are aimed at systems, not products.

Defaults and choice architecture

Studies of behaviour show that people often follow the default option. A household that is automatically enrolled in a green electricity plan behaves differently from one that must opt in, even though the personal "choice" is the same. This is a small example of a wider point: the options presented to people, and the defaults attached to them, are designed by someone, and those designers hold more influence over outcomes than any single customer.

7The structural problem

6.1 The profit motive

If destroying a forest is more profitable than preserving it, a business has an economic incentive to destroy it unless laws, regulation, workers or organised communities prevent it. This does not mean every business decision under capitalism causes environmental harm. It means profit incentives can conflict with long-term environmental interests.

6.2 Externalised costs

When environmental costs are not included in the price of production, they are "externalised". The sequence is simple: a company extracts a resource, earns a profit, pollution is produced, and the community and government deal with the consequences.

Benefits can concentrate among owners and investors while costs are spread across society. That raises the central question: who receives the benefits, and who pays the costs? They are not always the same group.

6.3 Concentrated power

A small number of large corporations can control significant parts of major supply chains. This limits purely consumer-based environmentalism: telling people to "just buy better products" assumes unlimited alternatives. If a few firms dominate an industry, changing individual purchases cannot by itself transform it.

The same logic applies to new technology. Artificial intelligence needs large amounts of computing infrastructure, data centres and electricity, and a small number of technology companies hold substantial control over those resources. Who decides how this infrastructure is built, and who absorbs the environmental cost?

6.4 Underfunded public goods

ApproachWhat changesWho acts
Buy a more efficient carOne household's vehicleThe individual consumer
Build reliable public transportThe infrastructure available to everyoneGovernments, planners, public funding
Key idea

Sometimes the most environmentally significant choices are not consumer choices at all. They are decisions about infrastructure, energy systems, land use and industrial policy.

6.5 Lock-in

Infrastructure lasts a long time. Power stations, road networks, pipelines and the layout of towns shape energy use for decades after they are built. Once a society is organised around a particular system, individual households have limited ability to leave it. This is one reason structural decisions about what to build matter more than any one purchase.

6.6 Who sets the rules?

Laws and regulations decide which costs a company must pay and which it can pass on. Research and reporting have documented substantial lobbying by fossil fuel and related industries on climate policy, although how much that lobbying changed outcomes is debated. The point for this report is that environmental outcomes depend on rule-making as well as on consumer behaviour, and that access to rule-making is itself unequal.

8The roots of the problem

A reel that inspired this project uses a tree as a metaphor. The visible problem is the tree above ground. The roots are the deeper systems supporting it, and cutting individual branches does not solve the problem if the roots remain intact. For example, a campaign can persuade people to use less plastic while the economic system that keeps producing disposable plastic is unchanged.

RootThe argumentA counterpoint
CapitalismProfit incentives can reward extraction when destroying is more profitable than preserving.Non-capitalist states have also caused severe environmental damage, and some argue regulated markets, such as carbon pricing, can cut emissions.
ImperialismCosts and resources are shifted across borders, often from poorer regions to richer ones.Historians and economists disagree about how much present-day extraction traces back to imperial structures.
Neoliberal policyDeregulation and privatisation weaken public control over resources.Supporters argue market reforms can fund growth and innovation that finance cleaner technology.
Weak labour organisationWhen workers and communities have less power, destructive projects are harder to stop.Some unions have opposed climate policy in carbon-intensive industries, which is why "just transition" policies matter.
Concentrated economic powerA few firms control major supply chains and the infrastructure behind them.Large firms can also invest at a scale in clean energy that small ones cannot.
Nonprofit industrial complexSome environmental work is shaped by funders and avoids confronting power.Many nonprofits win policy and legal victories, and funding sustains long campaigns.
Contested ideas

These arguments are political and contested. They are part of a broader debate, not universally accepted explanations of every environmental problem.

How to use the roots metaphor well

The tree image is useful, but it can be misused in two ways:

  • Naming one root as the only cause. Real environmental problems usually have several causes that vary by country, industry and period. Treating one root as the explanation for everything makes the argument weaker.
  • Using "the system" as an excuse. If every problem is blamed on deep structures, it can sound as if nothing can be done now. The argument of this report is the opposite: structural change is the goal, and individual and collective action are how it begins.

A careful use of the metaphor asks, for each environmental problem, which branches are visible, which roots support them, and who has the power to change those roots.

9Chico Mendes: environmentalism as class struggle

Chico Mendes was a Brazilian rubber tapper and labour organiser. He worked with rubber tappers in the Amazon who depended on the forest for their livelihoods. During the 1970s and 1980s he helped organise empates: non-violent collective actions in which rubber tappers and their supporters physically stood in the way of bulldozers and deforestation.

The struggle was not simply about saving trees. It was also about land ownership, workers' livelihoods, Indigenous and local communities, deforestation, economic power and control over natural resources. Mendes tied protection of the forest to the rights of the people who depended on it.

He was assassinated in 1988 by a rancher's gunman. A conservation area, the Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve, was later created in Brazil in his name, in 1990.

Key idea

Environmental conflicts can also be conflicts over land, labour and economic power. Protecting the forest and protecting the people who depended on it were not separate struggles.

Timeline

PeriodEvent
Mid to late 1970sMendes becomes active in the rural workers' union in Xapuri, in the Brazilian state of Acre.
1980sRubber tappers and supporters carry out empates to block forest clearing for cattle ranching.
1985The National Council of Rubber Tappers is founded and campaigns for extractive reserves.
1987Mendes travels abroad to press international lenders on the effects of road building and deforestation in the Amazon.
December 1988He is shot dead at his home in Xapuri.
1990The Chico Mendes Extractive Reserve is created in Acre.

Extractive reserves

The idea at the centre of the campaign was the extractive reserve: forest land that stays standing and is used collectively by the communities who gather products such as rubber and nuts from it, under legal protection. It tied conservation directly to livelihoods, so that people who depended on the forest had a reason, and a legal right, to defend it.

Why this is a class story

The conflict was between workers who used the forest and owners who wanted to clear it for profit. The forest was defended through collective organisation, not through individual consumer choices. That is the pattern this report argues is often missing when environmentalism is reduced to lifestyle.

10Why class matters

HouseholdTypical options
WealthyCan buy solar panels, an electric vehicle, efficient appliances and sustainable food.
Low-incomeMay struggle to afford basics and have little say over its energy source or transport.

Wealthier communities often have more political influence and resources to protect themselves from hazards. Poorer communities may be more exposed to pollution, flooding, extreme heat and unsafe infrastructure. Those with the least power to change environmental systems can be the ones most affected by their consequences.

Heat, floods and the cost of adapting

Exposure to climate harm depends on money as well as geography. Households with savings, insurance and secure housing can adapt more easily to heat, flooding and storms, for example by moving, repairing or installing cooling. Households without those resources may have to stay in unsafe areas or absorb losses that they cannot recover from.

GroupContribution to emissionsExposure and ability to adapt
High-income householdsTypically high, driven by flying, large homes and several vehicles.Generally more resources to adapt.
Middle-income householdsModerate, mostly from housing, transport and food.Mixed, depending on location and savings.
Low-income householdsTypically low per person.Often more exposed and with fewer resources to recover.

Within countries and between them

Carbon inequality exists between countries, but also within them. Researchers such as Lucas Chancel have found that large gaps in emissions exist inside both rich and poor countries. This matters because policy that treats all households in a country as equal can place the same burden on people with very different means.

Designing fair climate policy

  • Return the money: rebates or dividends can protect lower-income households when fuel or carbon is priced.
  • Invest in alternatives: affordable public transport and efficient housing reduce costs as well as emissions.
  • Target luxury emissions: higher charges on private jets and very high consumption affect those with the most room to change.
  • Protect workers: training, income support and new jobs in regions that depend on high-carbon industries.

Three lenses

  • Consumption: luxury emissions (frequent flying, large properties) are a choice; subsistence emissions (cooking, heating, commuting) are a necessity.
  • Production: workers do not choose which fuels a company burns; owners and investors direct where capital goes.
  • Impact: the communities that emit least are often hit first and have the fewest resources to recover.

Case study: the 2018 fuel tax protests in France

A fuel tax increase was planned as part of climate policy. Rural and working-class drivers, who relied on cars and had few alternatives, experienced it as an unfair burden, while wealthier households faced less pressure to change. The "yellow vest" protests began in November 2018 and the government backed down. Many draw the lesson that climate policy which ignores class can fail politically, while policy that shares costs fairly, through rebates, public transport and taxes on luxury emissions, is more durable.

11Individual action vs systemic change

Does individual action matter? Yes. Does it alone solve environmental problems? No.

One person canOne person cannot, alone
Reduce waste, consume less, conserve electricity, use public transport, support sustainable businesses.Redesign an electricity grid, build a public transport network, regulate an industry, change land ownership, control corporate supply chains, set national energy policy, or stop large-scale extraction.
Individual action matters, but it exists within systems that individuals do not control.
The combined approach

Individual responsibility + collective action + structural change is a more complete environmentalism than lifestyle change alone. The goal is not to make people feel guilty for every purchase, but to understand how behaviour, collective action, economic systems and political power fit together.

A range of actions

LevelExamplesReach
PersonalCutting waste, flying less, changing diet, using public transport.One household.
CommunityRepair cafes, shared energy projects, local food and transport groups.A neighbourhood or town.
Workplace and civicUnions, student groups, professional bodies, campaigns.An institution or industry.
PoliticalVoting, lobbying, protest, running for office.Laws and budgets.
StructuralEnergy systems, land ownership, industrial and transport policy.The whole society.

The levels build on each other. Personal action can lead into community action, which can lead into political action. The argument of this report is that the higher levels are the ones that most often get left out of the conversation.

12Common objections and replies

A strong argument should be tested against its best objections. Six common ones are set out below, each with a reply and the question that remains open.

ObjectionReplyOpen question
"This just blames companies instead of people."The argument is about proportion, not exemption. Individuals still have responsibilities, but the largest decisions are made at institutional level.What is the right balance of responsibility?
"Companies only supply what people want."Demand is partly shaped by advertising, design and infrastructure, and some goods are hard to avoid.How much of demand is shaped and how much is independent?
"Everyone should do their part."Agreed, and nothing here argues against it. The point is that personal action alone cannot rebuild energy or transport systems.Which actions have the largest effect?
"Markets have driven clean technology."True in many areas, such as the falling cost of solar power, though public funding and policy have also played a large part.What mix of markets and public action works best?
"Non-capitalist states pollute too."Yes, environmental damage has occurred under many economic systems. This supports looking at power and accountability, not one label.What makes any system accountable to those affected?
"Collective action is too slow; individuals can act now."Both can happen together, and personal action can build the habits and networks that collective action grows from.How can quick personal steps be linked to longer-term change?

13Regenerative by design

Instead of extracting resources and minimising the damage afterwards, systems can be designed to restore and regenerate the environments and communities they depend on. Sustainability is then not only about what individuals buy, but about how systems are designed and who gets to decide.

ElementWhat it looks like
Renewable energyPower systems built on clean sources.
Sustainable agricultureFood systems that sustain people and soil.
Public transportationShared infrastructure that reduces the need for a car per person.
Circular productionProducts and processes designed to keep waste out.
Restoring ecosystemsRepairing damaged environments, not only protecting what remains.
Strong labour protectionsWorkers able to refuse harmful work and share in the transition.
Community participationPeople affected by decisions take part in them.
Democratic decision-makingWho decides becomes a public question.
Equitable access to resourcesResources and benefits shared more fairly.

Examples in practice

Ideas of regeneration already appear in real policies and projects. These are illustrations of the principle, not complete solutions:

  • Extractive reserves in Brazil, where forest is protected and used collectively by local communities.
  • Public transport networks that reduce the need for each household to own a car.
  • Community energy projects in which local people own part of the generation they use.
  • Right-to-repair rules, introduced in some places, which make products easier to fix and keep in use.

What success could look like

  • Emissions fall, and the costs of the transition are not placed mainly on lower-income groups.
  • Workers and communities in high-carbon industries have a path to secure work.
  • People affected by a decision have a real say in making it.
  • Systems are built to restore land, water and communities, not only to reduce harm.

14Conclusion

Environmentalism is often reduced to "What can I do?" That is an important question, but it should not be the only one. We should also ask:

  1. Who controls production?
  2. Who owns the resources?
  3. Who profits from extraction?
  4. Who makes environmental decisions?
  5. Who suffers from pollution and climate change?
  6. Who has the ability to change the system?

A reusable bottle can be useful. Planting a tree can be useful. Recycling can be useful. But none of these should stop us asking the bigger question:

Why does the system require us to constantly repair environmental damage instead of preventing it at its source?

Real progress may require both changing how we live and changing the systems that shape how we live. That is where environmentalism becomes more than a lifestyle: it becomes a question of power, equality and collective action.

Key takeaways

  • The personal carbon footprint is a useful measurement, but it is an incomplete way to understand responsibility.
  • BP is widely cited as having popularised the personal footprint, and the framing moved attention from producers to consumers.
  • Emissions, and exposure to harm, are unequal across classes, which is why class belongs in the conversation.
  • Individual action and collective action are not opposites. Real progress needs both, with attention on who holds power over the systems.

15Discussion questions

These questions are meant for class discussion or further reading.

  1. Is a carbon footprint calculator helpful, misleading, or both?
  2. How should responsibility for emissions be divided between consumers and producers?
  3. Does it matter who first popularised an idea if the idea is useful?
  4. How much of what people buy is a free choice?
  5. Which of the six roots is the most convincing, and which is the least?
  6. What can the story of Chico Mendes teach modern climate campaigns?
  7. How can climate policy be designed so it does not fall hardest on lower-income households?
  8. What would a regenerative version of your own city or campus look like?
